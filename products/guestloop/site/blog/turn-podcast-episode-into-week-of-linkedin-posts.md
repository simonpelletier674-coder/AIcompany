# How to Turn a Podcast Episode Into a Week of LinkedIn Posts (Without It Reading Like Everyone Else's)

*By The GuestLoop team*

Most "turn your podcast into LinkedIn content" advice produces five posts that all say the same thing in slightly different fonts. Same takeaway, same structure, same "here's what I learned from my latest episode" opener. You can spot them scrolling past at 2x speed. The problem isn't that hosts aren't trying — it's that almost everyone extracts one thing from an episode (the best soundbite, usually) and then rewrites that one thing five ways. Five rewrites of one idea still reads as one idea.

The fix isn't a better rewrite. It's pulling five *different* things out of the same conversation, because a 40-minute interview actually contains five different kinds of material if you know where to look: a narrative arc, a claim that cuts against consensus, a set of discrete tactics, a sentence worth quoting verbatim, and an unresolved question. Below is the structure, plus a worked example for each post type so you can see exactly what "different" looks like in practice, not just hear it asserted.

**A note on the example:** everything below is drawn from a hypothetical episode we built for this article — a fictional B2B podcast called *The Pipeline Problem*, hosted by a fictional host, interviewing a fictional guest, Priya Nadella, VP of Revenue Operations at a mid-market SaaS company. No such episode exists. We built it to be realistic enough that the technique transfers directly to a real transcript.

## Why five posts from one episode usually collapse into one post

Here's the failure mode in slow motion. A host finishes an episode, feels good about one line the guest said — something like "most demo calls fail in the first ninety seconds" — and then writes five LinkedIn posts that are all, structurally, "here's that demo-call insight, restated." Post 1 opens with the quote. Post 2 opens with a question about the quote. Post 3 lists three reasons the quote is true. By post 4, even engaged followers have pattern-matched what's happening and started scrolling faster, because the *shape* of the post told them what was coming before the words did.

The fix is to treat the episode as raw material for five structurally different post types, each of which does a different job and reads differently even to someone skimming fast. That's the whole trick. Not five better hooks — five different jobs.

## The five-post structure

1. **The story post** — narrative arc, low information density, high emotional pull
2. **The contrarian take** — a claim that pushes against what most people in the space believe
3. **The listicle** — tactical, scannable, screenshot-and-save material
4. **The direct quote** — the guest's exact words, minimal framing, maximum credibility
5. **The engagement question** — deliberately open-ended, built to generate comments not just reactions

Post them across a week, roughly one every one to two days, in whatever order matches your show's actual publish rhythm. Order matters less than variety — the goal is that someone who saw Monday's post and Thursday's post wouldn't necessarily clock them as coming from the same episode.

## Post 1: The story post

This is the arc, not the insight. You're not summarizing what the guest said — you're narrating what happened to them, with a beginning, a turn, and a resolution. Story posts work because LinkedIn's feed rewards dwell time, and a narrative earns more of it than a stated fact.

**Worked example, from the hypothetical episode:**

> Eighteen months ago, Priya Nadella's sales team was closing 22% of qualified demos. She sat in on twelve calls in a single week to find out why, expecting to hear pricing objections or competitor comparisons.
>
> She heard something else. In eleven of the twelve calls, the prospect had visibly checked out by the ninety-second mark — before pricing, before the demo even really started. The reps were opening with a company overview slide. Every single time.
>
> She banned the overview slide. Reps now have to state the prospect's specific problem, in the prospect's own words, within the first ninety seconds, or the call is considered off track. Close rate on qualified demos: 41%, eighteen months later.
>
> The fix cost nothing. It took her a year to notice the problem was even there.

Notice what's absent: no "3 lessons," no bullet points, no direct pitch for anything. It reads like something you'd tell a colleague over coffee, because that's the register it's written in.

## Post 2: The contrarian take

This is the post that earns comments from people who disagree, which — on LinkedIn's engagement math — is usually worth more than agreement. Find the moment in the episode where the guest said something that cuts against what most operators believe, and lead with the disagreement, not a hedge.

**Worked example:**

> Unpopular opinion, courtesy of a RevOps leader I interviewed this week: your discovery call template is actively hurting your close rate.
>
> Priya Nadella's argument: a scripted discovery call optimizes for consistency across reps, which sounds good in a sales-enablement deck and is quietly terrible for conversion. The prospects who convert best are the ones who feel like they're in a real conversation, not being walked through a checklist they can half-hear the rep reading off.
>
> Her team's fix wasn't "get rid of the template." It was cutting it from fourteen required questions to four, and telling reps the rest of the call is theirs to run.
>
> Close rates went up. Rep confidence went up. The only thing that went down was how consistent the calls sounded in QA reviews — which, it turns out, nobody outside the sales-ops team actually cared about.
>
> Are you optimizing your discovery process for conversion, or for how it looks in a call review?

## Post 3: The listicle

This is where you go fully tactical — the part of the conversation that's genuinely useful as a checklist, formatted so someone can screenshot it. Resist the urge to make this a summary of the whole episode. Pick one specific system the guest described and lay it out step by step.

**Worked example:**

> A RevOps VP told me how her team audits a losing demo call. Stealing this system:
>
> 1. Pull the call recording and jump to the 90-second mark — not the start. That's the moment attention is won or lost.
> 2. Check who spoke first after minute one: the rep or the prospect. If it's still the rep, that's a red flag.
> 3. Count how many times the rep said "we" versus how many times they said "you." A losing call skews "we."
> 4. Note the exact minute the prospect asked their first question. No question by minute five usually means the call was already lost.
> 5. Compare against your last three won deals using the same four checks. The pattern shows up fast.
>
> Five minutes per call, and it surfaces problems a scorecard never catches.

## Post 4: The direct quote

The lowest-effort, highest-credibility post type — and the one people skip because it feels too simple. It isn't. A sharp, verbatim quote with almost no framing does something a paraphrase can't: it puts the guest's actual voice in front of your network, which is good for you (credible, specific) and good for the guest (visibly, attributably featured).

**Worked example:**

> "We spent a year optimizing the wrong ninety seconds of the call. Everyone was staring at the demo. Nobody was staring at the opening."
>
> — Priya Nadella, VP of Revenue Operations, on this week's episode of The Pipeline Problem, talking about what actually moved her team's close rate from 22% to 41%.
>
> Full conversation linked in comments.

Two sentences of context, one sentence of attribution, done. Don't over-caption it.

## Post 5: The engagement question

This post's entire job is starting a conversation, not delivering a conclusion. Take the most genuinely unresolved tension from the episode — a place where the guest raised a question they didn't fully answer, or where you disagreed with them on air — and put that tension in front of your audience without resolving it yourself.

**Worked example:**

> On this week's episode, my guest and I disagreed about something and never fully resolved it: should reps be allowed to go off-script on discovery calls, or does that just let the good reps get better while the average reps get worse?
>
> Her position: freedom helps everyone, because the structure was the problem.
> My position: freedom helps your best three reps and quietly tanks your worst five.
>
> Curious where B2B sales leaders land on this — genuinely unresolved for me.

## Sequencing across the week

Publish order matters less than most advice implies, but two things are worth being deliberate about. First, don't post the direct quote first — save it for mid-week, after the story post has already built some context, so the quote lands with more weight. Second, close the week with the engagement question, not open with it; a cold audience doesn't have enough context yet to have an opinion on an unresolved tension from an episode they haven't heard referenced yet.
