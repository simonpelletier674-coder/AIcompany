## Episode 2: Andrew McAfee — "Strategy Summit 2026: Who's Going to Succeed with AI?"

### LinkedIn Draft 1 — Story

There's a moment in our Strategy Summit conversation with MIT's Andrew McAfee that I keep replaying. Someone in the audience asked him, more or less, "who wins the AI era?" Instead of naming a sector or a company size, McAfee said the honest answer right now is that nobody fully knows — that we're at a genuine "nobody knows anything" moment in how this technology reshapes business.

That's not a hedge. Coming from someone who's spent two decades studying how technology actually changes organizations, it reads more like a warning against the confident takes flooding LinkedIn every day, including, probably, some of mine.

What he was more certain about: the gap between companies that adopt well and companies that don't is going to widen, fast. The uncertainty is about who ends up on which side of it. Worth sitting with, especially if your five-year plan currently reads like a certainty.

### LinkedIn Draft 2 — Contrarian / insight

Unpopular opinion, courtesy of MIT's Andrew McAfee on our Strategy Summit stage: cutting entry-level hiring because AI can now do the routine parts of the job might be the most expensive mistake a company makes in the next five years.

The logic sounds airtight in a budget meeting. Junior analysts do a lot of repeatable work. AI does repeatable work well now. Cut the junior headcount, keep the senior judgment, bank the savings.

McAfee's pushback: "How else are people going to learn to do the job except via on-the-job learning and training apprenticeship? That's how you learn to do difficult knowledge work — by helping somebody who's good at that with the routine stuff."

You're not just cutting cost. You're cutting the pipeline that produces the senior judgment you're planning to rely on later. Efficient this quarter. Expensive in 2031.

### LinkedIn Draft 3 — Listicle

On stage at our Strategy Summit, Andrew McAfee talked about what he calls "geek organizations" — the companies actually built to take advantage of a moment like this one. From his research, four norms show up again and again in the ones that pull ahead:

1. Science — settling arguments with evidence and testing, not seniority.
2. Ownership — pushing decision rights down to the people closest to the problem.
3. Speed — shipping fast and correcting in public rather than perfecting in private.
4. Cooperation — trusting people to do right by the group without a rulebook for everything.

None of these four are new. What's new is how brutally AI exposes the organizations that only pretend to run this way. The tool rewards the real thing and punishes the theater.

### LinkedIn Draft 4 — Quote the guest

Andrew McAfee, on why he thinks cutting entry-level roles because of AI is a mistake companies will come to regret — from our Strategy Summit conversation on IdeaCast:

Every leadership team weighing an AI-driven headcount plan should read that twice before finalizing anything. The routine work junior people do isn't just cost to be automated away — for a lot of them, it's the training ground. Automate all of it and you haven't just trimmed a budget line, you've quietly switched off the apprenticeship your own org depends on five years out.
